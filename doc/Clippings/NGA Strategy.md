## I would pursue two objectives simultaneously

**Objective 1:** Get the job.

**Objective 2:** Make DN interesting to the company as a capability that *they* can take to NGA.

The second is important. If the CEO sees DN as something that could help his company win or expand NGA work, **he has a reason to introduce DN rather than suppress it.**

### Don't lead with "I have a product"

In the CEO interview, I would first establish that you understand the problem they're trying to solve.

Then, when appropriate, say something along the lines of:

> "I've also been developing something on my own that may be relevant to this kind of work. It's called DoubleNaught. It's an SLM development environment aimed at making model development substantially more accessible and reproducible. If I join you, I'd be interested in seeing whether it could be useful in the NGA environment."

That's a much better opening than:

> "I have software. I'd like to sell it to NGA."

You're showing that **you are there to solve their problem first**.

# The CEO is actually the person I'd want to tell

The fact that you're meeting the CEO **before the technical people** may be advantageous.

A small contractor CEO is likely thinking about:

- How do I win this work?
- How do I differentiate my company?
- How do I retain valuable technical people?
- How do I get additional work from NGA?
- How do I compete against larger contractors?

DN potentially answers several of those.

So your pitch to the CEO isn't really:

> **"Buy DN."**

It is:

> **"I may have something that could make your company more competitive with NGA."**

That's a considerably more compelling proposition.

# But establish the IP boundary early

This is the part I would be very careful about.

Before you show them source code, architecture details, proprietary algorithms, etc., establish that:

**DN was developed independently by you and is your pre-existing intellectual property.**

You don't necessarily need to make a big legal production out of this during the first interview. But you should be careful about what you disclose and **not casually put DN into a work-for-hire context.**

If you eventually join them as an employee, the employment agreement may contain provisions concerning inventions and intellectual property. **Read those provisions before signing.**

If you instead work through your own LLC, the IP boundary can be much cleaner.

And there is a very good reason to think about this now: NGA itself has multiple mechanisms for working with commercial technology—including OTAs, bailments, SBIR, CRADAs and Partnership Intermediary Agreements. Its current capabilities document specifically describes a **bailment** as a way for NGA to test and evaluate a mature commercial product with an identified mission requirement and user. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.nga.mil&sz=128)National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency](https://www.nga.mil/assets/files/NGA_Capabilities_2025.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is almost exactly the conceptual model you want:

> **Your technology exists independently → NGA evaluates it → government decides whether it has value.**

# I would NOT try to bypass the contractor

This is probably the most important strategic point.

Suppose you get hired and then privately approach NGA saying:

> "By the way, my employer doesn't know this, but I have DN and NGA should buy it."

That could create a mess.

Instead, make the contractor **your channel into NGA**.

Imagine the CEO eventually saying:

> "We have this guy who has developed an interesting technology. We'd like to demonstrate it."

Now the contractor has an incentive to promote you.

And potentially:

**You + DN + contractor + NGA**

becomes a much stronger combination than:

**You vs. contractor vs. NGA.**

# Don't tell NGA everything at once

There's also a strategic reason to avoid giving away DN's internals.

### Therefore, my strategy would be:

**1\. Get through the CEO interview.**

Don't make DN the center of the interview.

**2\. Establish yourself as someone who understands AI/ML deeply.**

Your technical history should help considerably here.

**3\. Find out what the contractor is actually trying to accomplish for NGA.**

This is enormously important. You need to know the *problem*, not merely the job description.

**4\. Determine whether DN actually gives them an advantage on that problem.**

Don't force the fit.

**5\. Tell the CEO about DN as a potential company differentiator.**

Not as something you want them to buy.

**6\. Show a carefully selected demonstration.**

Preferably unclassified.

**7\. If the CEO is interested, propose a joint demonstration to NGA.**

Now the contractor becomes your ally.

**8\. Before serious technical disclosure or funded development, settle IP ownership and licensing in writing.**

That is the point where I'd get an attorney familiar with **federal contracting/IP**, rather than relying on a generic LLC attorney.
